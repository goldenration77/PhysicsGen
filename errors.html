Theory of Errors - Documentation

1. Types of Errors

A. Systematic Errors

Definition: Errors that consistently skew measurements in one direction due to flaws in the experimental setup, instrument calibration, or methodology.

Characteristics:

  • Repeatable and predictable.
  • Cannot be reduced by increasing the number of measurements.
  • Often arise from:
    • Instrument miscalibration (e.g., a scale that always reads 0.1g too high).
    • Environmental factors (e.g., temperature affecting a measurement).
    • Human bias (e.g., parallax error in reading a scale).

Example: A thermometer that always reads 2°C higher than the actual temperature.

Mitigation:

  • Calibrate instruments regularly.
  • Use correction factors.
  • Improve experimental design.

B. Random Errors

Definition: Unpredictable variations in measurements due to uncontrollable factors (e.g., environmental noise, human reaction time).

Characteristics:

  • Follow no fixed pattern but average out over multiple measurements.
  • Can be reduced by increasing the number of measurements.
  • Follow statistical laws (e.g., normal distribution).

Example: Slight variations in repeated measurements of the same object due to hand tremors.

Mitigation:

  • Take multiple measurements and average them.
  • Use more precise instruments.
  • Control experimental conditions.

2. Propagation of Errors

When performing calculations with measured quantities, errors propagate through the operations, affecting the final result.

A. Error Propagation Rules

For a function \( f(x, y, \dots) \), the uncertainty \( \Delta f \) can be approximated using partial derivatives:

Operation Formula
Addition/Subtraction \( f = x \pm y \implies \Delta f = \sqrt{(\Delta x)^2 + (\Delta y)^2} \)
Multiplication/Division \( f = x \cdot y \) or \( f = \frac{x}{y} \implies \frac{\Delta f}{f} = \sqrt{\left(\frac{\Delta x}{x}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{\Delta y}{y}\right)^2} \)
Powers \( f = x^n \implies \frac{\Delta f}{f} = n \cdot \frac{\Delta x}{x} \)
General Formula (for independent errors) \( \Delta f = \sqrt{\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} \Delta x\right)^2 + \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} \Delta y\right)^2 + \dots} \)

Example:

If \( f = x \cdot y \), where \( x = 5.0 \pm 0.1 \) and \( y = 3.0 \pm 0.2 \), then:

\( f = 5.0 \times 3.0 = 15.0 \)

\( \frac{\Delta f}{f} = \sqrt{\left(\frac{0.1}{5.0}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{0.2}{3.0}\right)^2} = \sqrt{0.0004 + 0.0044} \approx 0.068 \)

\( \Delta f = 15.0 \times 0.068 \approx 1.02 \)

Thus, \( f = 15.0 \pm 1.0 \).

3. Normal Law of Errors (Gaussian Distribution)

Random errors typically follow a normal distribution (bell curve), characterized by:

  • Mean (μ): The most probable value (true value in the absence of systematic errors).
  • Standard Deviation (σ): Measures the spread of data.
    • ~68% of measurements lie within \( \mu \pm \sigma \).
    • ~95% within \( \mu \pm 2\sigma \).
    • ~99.7% within \( \mu \pm 3\sigma \).

Probability Density Function (PDF)

The normal distribution is given by:

\( P(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma \sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}} \)

Standard Error (SE)

The standard deviation of the mean of a sample:

\( SE = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{N}} \)

where \( N \) is the number of measurements.

4. Standard and Probable Error

A. Standard Error (SE)

  • Measures the precision of the mean.
  • Smaller SE → more precise estimate of the true value.

B. Probable Error (PE)

  • Defined as \( 0.6745 \sigma \).
  • The range \( \mu \pm PE \) contains 50% of the data.
  • Historically used before computers made standard deviation more common.

Relation Between SE and PE

\( PE = 0.6745 \cdot SE \)

Key Takeaways

  • Systematic errors require fixing the experimental setup.
  • Random errors can be reduced by increasing measurements and improving precision.
  • Error propagation must be considered in calculations.
  • Normal distribution is fundamental in error analysis.
  • Standard error and probable error quantify uncertainty in the mean.

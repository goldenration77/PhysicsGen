Thermodynamic Potentials, \(T\,dS\) Equations, Maxwell Relations & Compressibility Identities

This note provides a systematic, self-contained treatment of the four primary thermodynamic potentials, the two \(T\,dS\) equations, the derivation of Maxwell’s relations, and the important identities connecting heat capacities and compressibilities.

1. Thermodynamic Potentials

A thermodynamic potential is a state function whose natural variables allow all thermodynamic information of a system to be obtained by differentiation. The four fundamental potentials are:

Internal Energy \(U\)
Natural variables: \(S, V, \{N_i\}\)
Fundamental relation: \(U = U(S,V,\{N_i\})\)
Enthalpy \(H\)
Definition: \(H = U + PV\)
Natural variables: \(S, P, \{N_i\}\)
Helmholtz Free Energy \(F\) (or \(A\))
Definition: \(F = U - TS\)
Natural variables: \(T, V, \{N_i\}\)
Gibbs Free Energy \(G\)
Definition: \(G = H - TS = U + PV - TS = F + PV\)
Natural variables: \(T, P, \{N_i\}\)

1.1 Differential Forms (Closed System, No Composition Change)

From the combined first and second laws: \[ dU = T\,dS - P\,dV \] The other potentials follow by Legendre transforms: \[ \begin{align*} dH &= T\,dS + V\,dP \\ dF &= -S\,dT - P\,dV \\ dG &= -S\,dT + V\,dP \end{align*} \] These are the fundamental thermodynamic relations (Gibbs equations).

1.2 Physical Meaning & Usefulness

  • \(U\) – total microscopic energy. Useful for constant-\(S\), constant-\(V\) processes (isolated systems).
  • \(H\) – heat absorbed at constant pressure (for reversible processes). Natural for constant-\(P\) processes.
  • \(F\) – maximum work obtainable at constant \(T,V\) (excluding \(PdV\) work). Useful for constant-volume, isothermal processes; also the generating function for the canonical ensemble.
  • \(G\) – maximum non-expansion work at constant \(T,P\). Criteria for spontaneous processes and equilibrium at constant \(T,P\): \(dG_{T,P} \le 0\). Central in chemistry (phase equilibria, chemical reactions).
Equilibrium criteria (for closed systems):
  • \(U\) minimum at fixed \(S,V\)
  • \(H\) minimum at fixed \(S,P\)
  • \(F\) minimum at fixed \(T,V\)
  • \(G\) minimum at fixed \(T,P\)

2. The \(T\,dS\) Equations

Starting from \(dU = T\,dS - P\,dV\) and expressing \(S = S(T,V)\) or \(S = S(T,P)\), one obtains the two fundamental \(T\,dS\) equations.

2.1 First \(T\,dS\) Equation (variables \(T,V\))

\[ dS = \left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial T}\right)_V dT + \left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial V}\right)_T dV \] Using Maxwell’s relation \(\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial V}\right)_T = \left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_V\) and the definition \(C_V = T\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial T}\right)_V\), \[ T\,dS = C_V\,dT + T\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_V dV \]

2.2 Second \(T\,dS\) Equation (variables \(T,P\))

\[ T\,dS = C_P\,dT - T\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_P dP \] These equations are extremely useful for calculating entropy changes and for deriving relations between heat capacities and equation-of-state derivatives.

3. Maxwell’s Relations

Maxwell’s relations follow directly from the equality of mixed second partial derivatives of the thermodynamic potentials (Schwarz’s theorem).

3.1 Derivation

From the four differentials:
Potential Differential Maxwell Relation
\(U(S,V)\) \(dU = T\,dS - P\,dV\) \(\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial V}\right)_S = -\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial S}\right)_V\)
\(H(S,P)\) \(dH = T\,dS + V\,dP\) \(\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial P}\right)_S = \left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial S}\right)_P\)
\(F(T,V)\) \(dF = -S\,dT - P\,dV\) \(\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial V}\right)_T = \left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_V\)
\(G(T,P)\) \(dG = -S\,dT + V\,dP\) \(\displaystyle\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial P}\right)_T = -\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_P\)
The last two are the most frequently used in applications involving equations of state.

3.2 Applications

  • Expressing entropy derivatives in terms of measurable quantities (thermal expansion, compressibility).
  • Deriving the difference \(C_P - C_V\).
  • Obtaining thermodynamic identities for real gases, solids and liquids.
  • Relating adiabatic and isothermal processes.

4. The Relation \(C_P - C_V = \dfrac{TV\beta^2}{\kappa_T}\)

(Here \(\beta\) denotes the isobaric volume expansivity; the notation \(\alpha\) is also common.)

Definitions: \[ \beta = \frac{1}{V}\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_P, \qquad \kappa_T = -\frac{1}{V}\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial P}\right)_T \] Derivation From the two \(T\,dS\) equations one can write expressions for \(C_P\) and \(C_V\): \[ C_P - C_V = T\left[\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial T}\right)_P - \left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial T}\right)_V\right] \] Using the cyclic relation and Maxwell’s relations, one obtains the general identity: \[ C_P - C_V = T\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_P\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_V \] Now express \(\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_V\) via the cyclic identity: \[ \left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_V = -\frac{(\partial V/\partial T)_P}{(\partial V/\partial P)_T} = \frac{\beta}{\kappa_T} \] Substitution immediately yields: \[ C_P - C_V = T V \beta \cdot \frac{\beta}{\kappa_T} = \frac{T V \beta^2}{\kappa_T} \]
This is an exact thermodynamic identity. For an ideal gas \(\beta = 1/T\) and \(\kappa_T = 1/P\), recovering the familiar result \(C_P - C_V = nR\).

5. Isothermal and Adiabatic Compressibilities

Isothermal compressibility \[ \kappa_T = -\frac{1}{V}\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial P}\right)_T \] Adiabatic (isentropic) compressibility \[ \kappa_S = -\frac{1}{V}\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial P}\right)_S \] \(\kappa_S\) is the relevant quantity for rapid (adiabatic) processes such as the propagation of sound waves.

6. Ratio of Compressibilities: \(\dfrac{\kappa_T}{\kappa_S} = \dfrac{C_P}{C_V}\)

Derivation Consider the Jacobian identity or, equivalently, the chain rule for the three variables \(P,V,S\): \[ \left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial P}\right)_S = \left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial P}\right)_T + \left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_P\left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial P}\right)_S \] A more systematic route uses the general thermodynamic identity between second derivatives: \[ \left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial P}\right)_S = \frac{C_V}{C_P}\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial P}\right)_T \] (This follows from expressing both heat capacities in terms of Jacobians of \(S\) and \(T\) with respect to \(V\) and \(P\).) Consequently: \[ \kappa_S = \frac{C_V}{C_P}\kappa_T \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \frac{\kappa_T}{\kappa_S} = \frac{C_P}{C_V} \]
Since \(C_P > C_V\) for all stable systems, it follows that \(\kappa_T > \kappa_S\). Physically, an isothermal compression is “softer” than an adiabatic compression because heat can leave the system, allowing a larger volume change for the same pressure increase.
Important corollary – speed of sound The speed of sound in a fluid is \[ c = \sqrt{\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial\rho}\right)_S} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\rho\,\kappa_S}} = \sqrt{\frac{C_P}{C_V}\frac{1}{\rho\,\kappa_T}} \]

1. Introduction to Heat Conduction

Heat conduction is the transfer of thermal energy through a material without bulk motion of the material itself. It occurs via microscopic collisions and diffusion of particles (atoms, molecules, free electrons). In solids—especially metals—conduction is highly efficient because free electrons carry energy rapidly.

The rate of heat flow depends on the temperature gradient, the cross-sectional area, and an intrinsic material property called thermal conductivity.

Key idea: Heat flows from hotter to colder regions. The steeper the temperature gradient and the higher the conductivity, the greater the heat flux.

2. Thermal Conductivity \(K\)

Thermal conductivity \(K\) (also written \(k\) or \(\kappa\)) quantifies how well a material conducts heat. It appears in Fourier’s law of heat conduction:

\[ \vec{q} = -K\,\nabla T \]

where \(\vec{q}\) is the heat flux vector (W m−2) and \(\nabla T\) is the temperature gradient (K m−1). The negative sign indicates heat flows opposite to the gradient (from hot to cold).

For one-dimensional steady flow through a slab of thickness \(L\) and area \(A\):

\[ \frac{dQ}{dt} = K A \frac{\Delta T}{L} \]

SI unit of \(K\) is W m−1 K−1.

3. Thermal Diffusivity \(D\)

Thermal diffusivity \(D\) (often denoted \(\alpha\)) measures how quickly a temperature disturbance propagates through a material. It is defined as

\[ D = \frac{K}{\rho\, c} \]

where \(\rho\) is density (kg m−3) and \(c\) is specific heat capacity (J kg−1 K−1). Units of \(D\) are m2 s−1.

Physical meaning: High \(K\) means heat flows easily; high \(\rho c\) means the material stores a lot of heat per unit volume. Diffusivity is the ratio of “how fast heat moves” to “how much heat is stored”.

Metals have high \(D\) (≈ 10−5–10−4 m2 s−1); insulators have low \(D\) (≈ 10−7 m2 s−1).

4. Fourier’s Equation of Heat Conduction

Combining Fourier’s law with energy conservation (continuity equation) yields the heat equation. In the absence of internal heat sources the temperature field \(T(\vec{r},t)\) obeys

\[ \frac{\partial T}{\partial t} = D\,\nabla^2 T \]

This is a parabolic partial differential equation. It is linear, so solutions can be superposed.

Derivation sketch

  • Heat flux \(\vec{q} = -K\nabla T\).
  • Energy balance on a small volume: rate of heat accumulation = −divergence of flux.
  • Accumulation = \(\rho c\,\partial T/\partial t\).
  • Hence \(\rho c\,\partial T/\partial t = \nabla\cdot(K\nabla T)\). For constant \(K\) this reduces to the diffusion equation above.

5. Solution for Rectilinear (One-Dimensional) Flow of Heat

Consider a long thin rod of uniform cross-section lying along the \(x\)-axis. Lateral surfaces are insulated, so heat flows only in the \(x\)-direction. The heat equation simplifies to

\[ \frac{\partial T}{\partial t} = D\frac{\partial^2 T}{\partial x^2} \]

Steady state

When \(\partial T/\partial t = 0\), we obtain \(d^2T/dx^2 = 0\), so temperature varies linearly:

\[ T(x) = T_0 + \frac{T_L - T_0}{L}x \]

Time-dependent solution – infinite rod

For an infinite rod initially at temperature \(T=0\) except for a heat pulse at \(x=0\), the fundamental solution is the Gaussian

\[ T(x,t) = \frac{Q}{\rho c\sqrt{4\pi D t}}\,e^{-x^2/(4Dt)} \]

where \(Q\) is the energy deposited per unit area.

Finite rod with fixed end temperatures

A rod of length \(L\) with ends held at \(T(0,t)=T_1\) and \(T(L,t)=T_2\), and arbitrary initial condition, can be solved by separation of variables. The general solution is a linear steady-state part plus a Fourier sine series that decays exponentially in time:

\[ T(x,t) = T_1 + (T_2-T_1)\frac{x}{L} + \sum_{n=1}^\infty B_n\sin\Bigl(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\Bigr)e^{-n^2\pi^2 Dt/L^2} \]

The coefficients \(B_n\) are determined by the initial temperature distribution.

6. Ingen-Hausz’s Experiment

In 1789 Jan Ingen-Hausz devised a simple comparative method to rank the thermal conductivities of different metals.

Apparatus & procedure

  • Several rods of identical length, diameter and surface finish but of different metals are coated with a thin layer of wax.
  • One end of each rod is inserted into a common heat source (e.g. a vessel of boiling water or a heated block) so that they all receive the same temperature at \(x=0\).
  • Heat conducts along each rod; the wax melts progressively from the hot end.
  • After a fixed time the length of the melted zone is measured. The longer the melted length, the higher the thermal conductivity.
Quantitative relation (approximate): For rods of the same cross-section and under similar heat-loss conditions the melted length \(l\) satisfies \[ l \propto \sqrt{K} \] more precisely, the temperature distribution in the presence of surface losses leads to an exponential decay whose characteristic length scales with \(\sqrt{K}\).
